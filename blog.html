What I learned building an explainable diabetes classifier

Aug 2026 6 min read

For my final year project, accuracy alone wasn't enough — the model also had to explain *why* it flagged a patient as high-risk. Here's what changed once I added LIME on top of our SVM and Random Forest models, and why an explanation panel ended up being the feature doctors cared about most in feedback sessions.

We started with the usual pipeline — clean the dataset, engineer features, train a few candidate models, pick the best performer. SVM and Random Forest both landed in a similar accuracy range, so the deciding factor became something we hadn't planned for: could we explain a single prediction to someone who isn't a data scientist?

That's where LIME came in. Instead of treating the model as a black box, LIME approximates its behaviour around one specific prediction, then ranks which input features pushed that prediction up or down. Wiring this into the Flutter front end meant every result screen could show not just "high risk" but a short list of the readings driving that call — glucose level, BMI, age, and so on.

The biggest lesson: explainability isn't a bolt-on feature, it changes how you evaluate the whole project. We started checking whether explanations made clinical sense, not just whether accuracy numbers looked good — and that caught a couple of features that were technically predictive but practically misleading.

Getting started with Flutter: a beginner's roadmap

The order I'd learn Flutter in if I were starting today — widgets first, state management second, and packages only once the basics feel automatic.

Start with layout widgets until Rows, Columns and Stacks feel automatic, then move to stateful widgets before touching any state-management package. Once "setState" starts to feel limiting on a real project, that's the right moment to reach for Provider or Riverpod — not before, or the extra abstraction just adds confusion.

CNN architectures compared: AlexNet vs VGG16 vs VGG19

Notes from training four CNN architectures on the same sports-ball dataset — where the extra depth of VGG helped, and where it just meant slower training.

AlexNet trained fastest and got us a working baseline within an hour. LeNet, being much shallower, struggled once the image classes got visually similar. VGG16 and VGG19 closed that gap, but the deeper VGG19 barely improved on VGG16 despite costing noticeably more training time — a reminder that more layers isn't automatically a better trade-off for a small dataset.

From raw CSV to insight: my data-cleaning checklist

The five checks I run on every new dataset during my Data Science & Analytics internship, before a single model gets trained.

In order: check for missing values and decide impute vs. drop per column, check data types against what each column actually represents, scan for duplicate rows, look at outliers with a boxplot before deciding whether they're errors or real signal, and finally confirm class balance for anything I'll later classify. Doing this in the same order every time has saved me from more than one bad model.
